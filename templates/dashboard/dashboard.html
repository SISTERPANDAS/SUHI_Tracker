Core Thermal Indices & Urban Dynamics Guide

As cities grow, human infrastructure replaces natural landscapes with concrete, asphalt, and buildings. This drastically alters local microclimates and creates unique heat patterns. To explore these transformations, our platform tracks multi-layered thermal and spatial dynamics over extended historical periods:

SUHI Intensity
  • What it is: Urban environments absorb and retain significantly more solar radiation than surrounding rural landscapes.
  • SUHI Intensity: Measures temperature difference ($\Delta T$) between densely built-up city centers and cooler rural baselines.
LULC Classification
  • What it is: Categorization of Earth's surface features and land utilization.
  • Features: Categorizes pixels into 4 colors: Water (blue), Vegetation (green), Built-up (red), and Barren land (yellow).
LST Retrieval
  • What it is: Radiative "skin" temperature tracking how hot the ground or pavement feels.
  • Retrieval: Uses thermal infrared bands while correcting for atmospheric interference.
Advanced Mathematical & Spatial Metrics
•
Annual Growth Rate (AGR): Measures percentage expansion speed of built-up zones.
•
Centroid Migration: Tracks the directional shift of the city's geographical center over decades.
•
Pixel-Level Correlation (r): Evaluates statistical links between impervious surfaces and rising temperatures.
Key Takeaway: Combining high-resolution land cover mapping with multi-temporal temperature timelines helps urban planners design data-driven cooling policies.

What is Landsat?

The Landsat Program is the longest-running enterprise for the acquisition of satellite imagery of Earth. Operated jointly as a partnership between NASA and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS), Landsat launched its first satellite (Landsat 1) in July 1972. To date, a total of 9 Landsat satellites have been dispatched into space (spanning from 1972 to present, utilizing modern series like Landsat 8 and 9 for ongoing data collection). Together, this series provides the world's most continuous space-based record of how our planet's land surface, cities, forests, and water bodies have changed over the decades.

Landsat Series Used (2000–2025):
  • Landsat 5 (1984–2013): Utilized for the early 2000s baseline data.
  • Landsat 7 (1999–2025): Covered the turn of the millennium through mid-2025 analyses.
  • Landsat 8 (2013–Present): Provided primary high-resolution optical and thermal data for the 2010s and 2020s.
  • Landsat 9 (2021–Present): Used alongside Landsat 8 for modern, high-precision thermal infrared and surface tracking.
Pixel Resolution & Bands: How Satellites "See" Heat and Land

Landsat satellites use specialized multispectral optical and thermal sensors that capture different wavelengths of light—many of which are invisible to the human eye.

30-Meter Pixel Size:

Most standard optical bands (like Red, Green, Blue, and Near-Infrared) capture data at a spatial resolution of 30 meters. This means every single pixel in the image represents a 30m × 30m square on the ground (roughly the size of a baseball infield).

Catching Light and Heat :

Satellites flying high above Earth take special photographs using different "lenses" to see things our eyes cannot catch on their own.

  • Optical Bands: These act like a normal camera lens that records sunlight bouncing off the ground. By comparing red light and near-infrared light, scientists can measure how healthy and thick the plant life is in a city.
  • Thermal Infrared Bands: These act like an invisible heat-vision camera. They measure the actual warmth radiating up from roofs, roads, and soil, which helps scientists track where cities are getting dangerously hot.
Flexible Region Selection Methods

When starting your urban heat and expansion analysis, you need to tell the platform where you want to look. You can easily define your study area using any of these three beginner-friendly methods:

1. Major City Dropdown

What it is: A pre-configured list of major Indian metropolitan areas and urban centers.

How to use it: Simply open the dropdown menu, select a city, and the platform will automatically load its boundary and geographic coordinates so you can start analyzing right away.

2. Coordinate Inputs

What it is: A precise, numbers-based location finder.

How to use it: Type in the exact Longitude and Latitude values of a specific location. The platform will automatically generate a center buffer zone around those coordinates to target your exact local region of interest, or you can type in the border coordinates of your desired region and the system will draw the borders for you.

3. Custom Drawing

What it is: An interactive map tool that lets you draw your own boundaries.

How to use it: Use the map drawing toolbar on the dashboard to click and outline custom polygons or rectangles around any exact neighborhood, district, or study zone you want to investigate.
